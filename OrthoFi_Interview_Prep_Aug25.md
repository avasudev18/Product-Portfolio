## 2. Domain cheat sheet — learn this vocabulary cold

You should be able to use these terms unprompted and correctly. Nothing signals "I did the work" faster; nothing signals the opposite faster than fumbling them.

**The conversion funnel you'd own:**
New patient inquiry → scheduled consult → in-office exam → **treatment presentation** → **financial presentation** → signed agreement → **down payment** → treatment start (a "start"). OrthoFi calls conversion at the end of this funnel **case acceptance**, and the practice-side outcome is **starts**.

**Case acceptance rate** = accepted treatment plans ÷ presented treatment plans. Reported industry averages vary by source; OrthoFi's own blog cites roughly 40–50% as typical, with high performers near 68%. Know the range, don't be dogmatic about a single number.

**The people:**
- **Treatment coordinator (TC)** — the staff member who presents treatment and financials to the patient. This is your primary user. The JD explicitly asks you to understand what it feels like to be this person. Learn their day.
- **Office manager / practice owner (the orthodontist)** — economic buyer, cares about starts, cash flow, and staff time.
- **The patient** — often a parent deciding for a child. High-stakes, infrequent, emotionally loaded, and price-sensitive.

**The products:**
- **OrthoFi Yes!** — patient acquisition, released as a standalone product.
- **AcceptCare** — case presentation plus financing. Per OrthoFi's materials it uses a **payment slider** where the patient sets a down payment and monthly amount within their budget and sees pre-qualified offers, a **multi-lender network** with **soft credit checks** that don't affect the patient's score, terms extending to 72 months, and **automated SMS/email follow-up** starting a day after presentation and repeating weekly for up to 90 days. OrthoFi reports that roughly a quarter of patients accept or pay from home within 30 days post-consult.
- **OrthoBanc** — patient accounts receivable and collections; acquired, and the reason lender relationships appear in the JD.
- **CFI (Comprehensive Finance)** — acquired 2021, the financing arm.

**The economic tension you must be able to articulate.** This is the heart of the role, and I'd expect Aaron to probe it directly:

> A lower down payment and a longer term increases the odds the patient says yes — but reduces upfront cash to the practice and raises the risk the patient stops paying before treatment completes. A higher down payment protects cash and collection but loses starts. The right answer isn't a fixed policy; it's a per-patient offer that maximizes expected value across acceptance, cash flow, and completion.

If you can say that fluently and unprompted, you have neutralized most of the "financial fluency" concern in the JD. That sentence is your single highest-value piece of preparation.

**Where the AI opportunity actually lives** (have a view ready — see §6): offer optimization per patient, insurance verification before the visit, follow-up prioritization for undecided patients, and default-risk signals that inform terms rather than gate approval.

## 5. Questions to prepare for

### The scope and leadership questions

**"Tell me about the team you led at PhaseZero."**
Have ready: five people, the mix of roles, roughly three years, and one specific coaching story — a person who got better because of something you did. Name what you changed in *how they worked*, not what you assigned them. This is the JD's stated bar: making PMs better, not directing them. If you have an example of giving someone difficult feedback that they initially resisted, that's the strongest possible version.

**"Describe a strategy you set and the business result it produced."**
Your PhaseZero churn/retention work is the best candidate: reactive escalation → predictive intervention → ~75% retention improvement. Structure it as: what the number was, why it was that way, what you decided to bet on, what you deliberately chose *not* to do, and what happened. The "what I chose not to do" is what separates a Director answer from a Senior PM answer.

**"How do you decide what not to build?"**
Have a real example where you killed or deferred something. Your empty-state decision on MaintenanceGuard's recommendation engine works: you had a shippable feature and decided the trust cost exceeded the value.

### The discovery question — expect this, the JD is emphatic about it

**"Walk me through how you'd run discovery on a consequential bet."**
The JD says: "rigorous discovery that tests the riskiest assumptions before committing to build." Answer in that vocabulary. Name the riskiest assumption first, describe the cheapest test that could falsify it, and say what evidence would make you walk away. Your gated beta — 15 users, allowlist, feedback into a prioritized roadmap — is a concrete example of testing before scaling.

### The domain question

**"What do you think drives case acceptance?"**
Do not answer this as a pricing question, which is the obvious trap. The better answer, which OrthoFi's own content supports: case acceptance is largely determined before the financial conversation starts — insurance verified ahead of the visit, the coordinator prepared, the plan framed as an outcome rather than a number — and then the offer structure either removes the last barrier or creates one. Flexibility in how the down payment and monthly amount are set matters more than the headline price. Then add the tension from §2 about cash flow and completion risk.

### The AI question — he will ask this

See §6. It's important enough to have its own section.

## 6. Your AI point of view — build this, don't improvise it

The JD asks for someone who "thinks clearly about where machine learning creates compounding leverage and where it is the wrong tool," and calls out that OrthoFi's data richness across thousands of practices creates conditions for applied intelligence. Aaron will test whether your AI fluency is real or vocabulary.

**Your strongest single story:** the MaintenanceGuard recommendation engine. You had probabilistic output that wasn't reliable enough, and you shipped an honest empty state rather than degrade user trust. Tell it in thirty seconds and land the principle: *the cost of a confidently wrong answer is not symmetric with the value of a right one, and in any product where the user is making a financial commitment, that asymmetry should decide whether you ship.*

That principle transfers directly to their domain. A wrong maintenance recommendation annoys someone. A wrong financing recommendation to a parent deciding on their child's treatment is a different order of harm — and in a business touching lending, it's also a compliance surface.

**Where you'd look for leverage at OrthoFi** (offer two or three, framed as hypotheses you'd test, not conclusions — the JD says "grounded, not speculative"):

- **Per-patient offer optimization.** With thousands of practices and millions of patients, there is enough history to model the joint outcome of acceptance, upfront cash, and completion for a given offer structure. That's a genuine compounding-data advantage that a rules engine cannot reach.
- **Follow-up prioritization.** Undecided patients get a fixed automated cadence. Which ones are actually reachable, and what would change their mind, is a ranking problem worth more than a longer drip sequence.
- **Pre-visit preparation.** Insurance verification and benefit calculation before the patient arrives is largely extraction and reconciliation — a good fit for models, and it moves an upstream driver of acceptance.

**Where you'd say AI is the wrong tool.** Have one ready. Suggested: anything that determines whether a patient is *approved* for financing. Adverse-action explainability and fair-lending exposure mean a probabilistic model shouldn't be the decision-maker there — it can inform which offers to surface, but the approval logic needs to be auditable. Saying this unprompted will land harder than any capability you list.

**On your own AI use:** the JD asks for someone who uses AI actively in their own work, not just builds with it. Be ready to say concretely how you use it — drafting and pressure-testing specs, research synthesis, prototyping. Specifics only.

## 7. The three hard questions

Rehearse these more than the easy ones. Each has a version that hurts you and a version that helps.

### "You haven't worked in consumer lending or credit risk. How would you lead strategy here?"

**Don't:** stretch the PayPal and Authorize.Net integrations into financial fluency. Those are payment processing. He ran product at EverCommerce; he will know the difference within one follow-up question, and the recovery cost is high.

**Do:** concede the specific gap cleanly, then demonstrate that you already understand the *shape* of the problem. Something like — "I haven't owned a lending relationship or a credit policy, and I'd be learning that. What I do have is experience owning the economics of an offer: at MaintenanceGuard I designed the tier structure where what we put in front of the customer determined whether they converted, and at PhaseZero I owned a portfolio where I was accountable for the revenue number, not the feature list. And I understand the tension in your domain — that a lower down payment buys acceptance at the cost of upfront cash and completion risk, and the job is optimizing across all three rather than tuning one." Then ask him a real question about it. Conceding and then out-thinking the concession is the strongest available move.

### "This would be your first Director title. Why are you ready?"

**Do:** point at scope, not title. Five direct reports across mixed disciplines, a $20M ARR platform, a domain you owned end-to-end, executive-level customer conversations you ran alone. Then be honest about what's new: the JD describes leading a small team of high performers where the bar is explicitly high, and you should say what you'd do in the first 90 days to earn that team's confidence — one-on-ones, understanding what each person is working on and why, and writing one brief yourself on the most consequential open question so they see your standard rather than hear about it. That last part maps directly to the JD's "Sets the Standard" bullet.

### "Tell me about MaintenanceGuard. Why are you leaving it?" / "Is this a real company?"

**Don't:** get defensive or over-explain. Fifteen beta users is fifteen beta users.

**Do:** own the scale plainly and sell the learning. "It's a real product with real users and it's small. What it's given me is a year of making every decision myself — pricing, architecture, what to cut, when the AI output wasn't good enough to ship. I'd rather bring that to a domain with 2,000 practices behind it than keep optimizing something at this scale." To a three-time founder, this reads as maturity. Also decide in advance what you'll say about whether you'd continue it on the side, because he may ask, and a wobbly answer there creates a commitment concern.

## 8. Questions to ask Aaron

Pick four. Good questions here do double duty — they demonstrate domain thinking while getting you real information.

1. "When you look at the Practice Growth funnel today, where's the biggest drop-off — getting the consult scheduled, or getting to yes after the financial presentation? And which one do you think is more addressable?"

2. "The JD frames the financial offer as one lever inside a broader conversion experience. Is the team's current center of gravity on the offer side or the experience side, and are you looking to shift it?"

3. "What does the team look like right now — how many PMs, and what's the biggest thing standing between them and the bar you want?"

4. "You've built product orgs at EverCommerce and DaySmart, both vertical SaaS serving small businesses. What's different about orthodontics that surprised you?" *(This one shows you did the work on him, not just the company. Use it.)*

5. "How does OrthoFi decide between what's best for the practice's cash flow and what's best for patient access when those conflict?"

6. "What would have to be true twelve months from now for you to say this hire went well?"

Avoid asking about comp, hybrid logistics, or the reporting structure in this round.
